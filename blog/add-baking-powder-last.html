When to Add Baking Powder Last (and Why You Usually Don't Have To)

Most of the time, baking powder goes in with everything else, even for a batter that rests in the fridge overnight. Here's why a good gluten-free batter holds onto the rise, and the rare case where I add it last.

The short answer
  • Most batters: the baking powder goes in with everything else, even if the batter rests in the fridge overnight.
  • As it rests, the starches, fibers and proteins set into a gel that holds the gas until it hits the oven.
  • Rare exception: a batter that's too wet loses its bubbles overnight. For those, rest it without the baking powder, then mix it in the next day and bake right away.
  • Switched to double-acting and they collapse? Use about 25% less.

I get asked about this one constantly, so here's exactly what I do. At the bakery, the baking powder goes into the batter with everything else, and the batter rests in the fridge overnight. Adding it last is a fix I save for the stubborn bakes, not something I do every time.

A well-built gluten-free batter holds onto its baking powder. Adding it last is the backup plan, not the rule.

Why a rested batter keeps its rise

Baking powder starts releasing CO₂ as soon as it gets wet, so it's fair to worry that an overnight rest burns it all off. What stops that is the batter itself. While it rests, the starches and the right fibers and proteins (psyllium, flax, and the proteins in your flours) soak up the liquid and set into a gel network. That gel is thick enough to trap the bubbles, so the gas stays in the batter overnight instead of escaping.

Then the oven does the rest. Double-acting baking powder gives a second burst of gas when it heats up, and the rested batter is already built to hold it. That's how you get a tall, even crumb from a batter that sat in the fridge all night.

When I add the baking powder last

Every so often a batter comes out too wet. The gel is too loose to hold the gas, so the baking powder bubbles up in the fridge, and by the next day a lot of those bubbles are gone. It's rare, but when it happens, I don't put the baking powder in the night before. I wait.

1. Mix and rest the batter without baking powder

Make the batter as usual, minus the baking powder. Cover it and let it rest in the fridge overnight so the flours hydrate.

2. Stir the baking powder into a little flour

The next day, take the batter out and mix your measured baking powder into 1–2 tablespoons of flour. Never dump it straight into wet batter. The flour spreads it out so you don't get bitter pockets.

3. Mix it in for 30–45 seconds

Add it to the batter and mix on medium for 30–45 seconds. That's enough to spread it evenly without knocking out the air.

4. Straight into the pan and the oven

Don't let it sit once the baking powder is in. Get it into the pan and into a preheated oven right away.

Also Switching to Double-Acting? Read This.

If your muffins spring and collapse after switching baking powders, you have a second problem on top of timing.

Double-acting baking powder produces more total CO₂ than single-acting. If your recipe was calibrated for single-acting, you're now over-leavening. There are too many bubbles and the structure can't hold them, so it collapses on cooling. Adding it later won't fix a volume problem. You need to reduce the amount too. Try cutting your double-acting amount by 25% - so if you would use 1 tsp single-acting, use ¾ tsp double-acting. Try baking a test batch to see the results. You should get a clean dome with no collapse after full cooling. If they still sink, drop another 10–15% and test again.

If you're still dealing with collapse, other possible causes include:

  • Oven running low
  • Cups overfilled past ⅔
  • Oven door opened before structure sets
